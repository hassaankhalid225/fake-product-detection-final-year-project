CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

1.1 Background
In the 21st century, the complexity of global supply chains has increased exponentially. A typical electronic gadget might have components manufactured in ten different countries, assembled in an eleventh, and sold in a twelfth. This complexity is a 'blind spot' for security. Counterfeiters exploit these gaps to inject fake components or finished goods into the stream of commerce. The economic impact is estimated at 3.3% of total world trade. However, the human cost is even higher. Fake medicines, for instance, lead to hundreds of thousands of deaths every year. Traditional tracking systems like barcodes and holograms are no longer sufficient as they are easily duplicated by professional counterfeiters.

1.1.1 Specific Industry Impact: Sector 1
The impact of counterfeiting on this sector is particularly severe. Studies show that consumers are often unaware that they are purchasing fakes until the product fails. This leads to a loss of brand equity for the original manufacturer. Furthermore, legal liabilities arise when fake parts cause accidents. The need for an immutable, globally accessible record is clear. Centralized databases managed by single companies are not trusted by all participants in a cross-border supply chain. Blockchain provides the necessary 'Trustless' environment.

1.2 Problem Statement
Current anti-counterfeiting measures fail because they rely on 'security through obscurity'. Holograms are hard to copy, but once a counterfeiter masters the technique, they can produce millions of fakes. Centralized databases are a 'black box' for the consumer. There is no transparency. If a corrupt employee at a logistics company alters a record in a central DB, the fake becomes 'real' on paper. We need a system where records cannot be deleted or modified once they are written. We need a system where every participant can audit the history of a product without needing permission from a central authority.

1.3 Objectives
1. To develop a decentralized ledger for product provenance.
2. To implement smart contracts that enforce ownership rules.
3. To create a public verification interface for consumers.
4. To reduce administrative costs in the supply chain.
5. To ensure 100% data integrity using cryptographic hashing.
6. To provide real-time tracking for manufacturers.
7. To eliminate the need for specialized hardware in verification.
8. To create a scalable architecture for millions of products.

2.1 Comparative Analysis of Existing Work
Literature review reveals three generations of anti-counterfeiting. First generation: Physical markers (Stickers, Holograms). Second generation: Electronic markers (RFID, NFC). Third generation: Blockchain-based digital twins. While RFID is efficient, its cost inhibits adoption for low-margin goods. Blockchain, however, offers a low-cost transactional model that can be applied to any item with a unique serial number.
